leg pain going down past the knee, no bowel or bladder problems, no fevers, no 
weight loss. Did you read my pain log, or just the summary they sent over? 
Because the log says the bad days line up with lifting and long drives, not 
with anything the imaging says.
Dr. Renata Okafor-Lindqvist: Okay, that's useful, and I'll be straight with 
you: a disc bulge at L4-L5 with mild foraminal narrowing on a 2019 MRI, normal 
neuro exams, no radicular pain past the knee, no red flags. That picture is 
common, and in plenty of people the bulge on imaging isn't what's producing the
pain. Your log pointing to lifting and long drives is more informative than the
scan. Bad days tracking mechanical load suggests a mechanical problem we can 
work on.

I haven't seen the pain log itself, only what you've told me, so I'd like to 
read it before I put weight on it. Bring the actual log to the next visit. And 
the one thing I need to know now: has anyone tried a structured course of 
physical therapy focused on load management and hip and core strength, for at 
least six weeks, with you actually doing it? Because if the answer is no, 
that's what I'd try before anything new, and I'd want to see the same log at 
the end of it.
Denise Okafor-Brandt: Yes, somebody tried it. I did PT for eleven weeks in 2022
with a therapist I liked, and I went to nearly every session. We worked on hip 
hinge mechanics, core stabilization, and graded lifting. It helped on the good 
days and did nothing for the bad ones. Then my insurance stopped covering it 
past twenty visits, and I couldn't keep paying out of pocket, so I stopped.

So don't tell me to try it again like it's new. What I want to know is why you 
think the second round will be different from the first. And if it's not, 
what's the next step after PT that doesn't just mean another pill or a knife?
Dr. Renata Okafor-Lindqvist: Fair. I won't tell you to repeat eleven weeks that
already gave you partial relief and didn't touch the bad days. I'd only expect 
a different result if something changes in how it's done, not just the 
calendar.

So here's what I'd want to know before anything else: did the program on the 
bad days ever get modified? Eleven weeks of hinge and core work that helps good
days and not bad ones usually means the load on bad days wasn't being managed, 
the lifting and long-drive pattern itself. Did anyone have you working on what 
happens the day before a long drive, or how you split a heavy load, or 
positioning in the car?

If the answer is no, the next step isn't another pill or a knife. It's a 
targeted, short episode: a few visits focused on your specific triggers, a plan
for flare days, and a seated-position and driving fix, measured against your 
log. If that also fails, then we're looking at a diagnostic block (a targeted 
injection at L4-L5, which tells us whether that level is the generator) before 
any surgical conversation. That's the honest order. Do you know whether anyone 
ever looked at the driving piece, or was it all done on the table?
Denise Okafor-Brandt: Some of it was done in the clinic. Driving got one 
conversation, maybe five minutes, about moving the seat closer and taking 
breaks. Nobody asked what I was doing the day before a drive, or how I was 
carrying the lifts at work. I'd have to tell you I was asked that once by a 
receptionist, when she wanted my insurance card updated.

Here's my problem with the injection. You said it tells us whether L4-L5 is the
generator. Fine. But I've billed enough of those procedures to know a positive 
block can be read a dozen ways, and the first one is usually "the procedure 
worked, so do the next one." So before I agree to a needle in my spine, tell me
what result would make you say L4-L5 is NOT the problem, and what you'd do 
then. If you can't answer that, I'm not sure the test is for me.
Dr. Renata Okafor-Lindqvist: Good question, and the honest answer is that a 
diagnostic block is only worth doing if it can come back negative and change 
the plan. So here's the decision rule, set before the needle goes in.

Two separate injections, not one. The first is a medial branch or facet block, 
or a selective nerve root block, depending on what the imaging and exam point 
to. We need to agree on which one before we do it. We diagnose the level with 
local anesthetic only, no steroid. If you get near-complete relief of your 
typical bad-day pain for the duration of the anesthetic, that's a positive. If 
your pain is unchanged, or you're still at baseline after a fair observation 
window, that's a negative for L4-L5 as the main generator.

If it's negative, I'd stop chasing L4-L5. We'd look elsewhere: the sacroiliac 
joint, the hip, or we'd accept that the bulge is incidental and go back to the 
load-management problem, which your log already points to. No further 
injections at that level, and no surgical discussion on the strength of this 
test.

If it's positive, I still wouldn't jump to the next procedure automatically. A 
positive anesthetic block earns a single steroid-based treatment at most, with 
a defined re-assessment at about six weeks against your log, using the same 
measures. Only if that yields a durable, meaningful improvement do we talk 
about repeating it, and never open-ended.

So tell me what you'd accept as a meaningful improvement. Write the number down
now, before the procedure, so neither of us can move the goalposts later.

canceling errands on at least four of those. A meaningful improvement is bad 
days dropping to two or fewer out of ten over a four-week stretch, and at least
half as many missed days. Anything less and I'd call it noise.

And since you're asking me to commit, I'll ask you the same. If I hit that 
number at six weeks, will you say so in writing, and will you tell the next 
doctor it happened? Because I've watched a "good response" get lost in a chart 
before, and it usually gets lost in the direction that justifies the next 
procedure.
