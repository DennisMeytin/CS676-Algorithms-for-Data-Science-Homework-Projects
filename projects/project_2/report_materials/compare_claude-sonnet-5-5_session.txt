── conversation: They argue about the diagnosis of Dale's chronic back pain. ──

Dr. Renata Okafor-Lindqvist: Okay, here's where we are. You're telling me it's 
just a bad back from years of lifting, and you may be right. Most chronic back 
pain is exactly that. But "most" is how people get missed, and I'm not calling 
it mechanical until I've asked a few things.

One, does it wake you at night, or hurt when you're lying still? Two, any 
weight loss, fevers, or numbness in your legs? Three, any change in bowel or 
bladder habits?

If the answer to all three is no, I'll happily agree with you. If it's yes to 
any of them, we're getting imaging today, and I don't want to hear about it 
being a hassle.
Dale Kowalczyk: Three questions. Fine, that's more than the last guy asked.

No fevers. No weight loss, unless you count the twenty pounds I'd happily lose 
on purpose. Legs go a little tingly now and then, but that's been there since 
the fusion. Bathroom's fine.

Nights... I don't sleep great. Everybody my age says that. Hurts some when I 
lie down, sure. Whether it wakes me up, I'd have to think about that.

And I'll say this now. Last time somebody ordered imaging on me, it ended with 
a surgeon telling me "ninety percent better." So forgive me if I don't get 
excited about pictures.
Dr. Renata Okafor-Lindqvist: Okay, here's where we are. I'm not hearing red 
flags, but I'm not hearing a clean "no" either. Pain when you lie still is the 
one that bothers me, and "I'd have to think about it" tells me it's worth 
pinning down.

Two things. First, the tingling: has it changed at all since the fusion? Same 
spots, same frequency, or is it creeping? "Been there for years" is fine. "Been
there for years and lately a little more" is not. Second, think about this past
week. Have you woken up because of the pain, or just woken up and noticed it?

On the imaging, I hear you. An MRI is a picture, not a contract. Nobody is 
signing you up for a knife because of a scan, and that "ninety percent better" 
line is a promise I wouldn't make either. If I order one, it's to rule out 
something specific, and you'll know what before you go.

Answer those two and I'll make the call.
Dale Kowalczyk: Tingling's in the same spots. Left foot, outside of the calf. 
Maybe it's a little more than last winter, but that could be the cold. Could be
a lot of things.

Nights. Okay. Yeah, it wakes me up. Two, three times this week. I end up on the
floor with the heating pad because the mattress is no good.

I wasn't going to say that. But you asked straight, so there it is.

As for the scan, I'll think about it. Tell me what you're looking for first, 
and I'll decide after that.
Dr. Renata Okafor-Lindqvist: Thank you. That's the answer I needed, and I know 
it wasn't the one you wanted to give.

Okay, here's where we are. Night pain that wakes you, plus tingling that may be
creeping, in a back that's already been fused. That combination earns a scan. 
It doesn't mean something's wrong. It means I can't call it mechanical on a 
maybe.

Here's what I'm looking for:

One, a problem next to your fusion. The discs above or below a fused level take
extra strain, and they can pinch a nerve. That fits your left calf and foot, 
and it's common and fixable.

Two, a problem with the hardware itself, like loosening or a healing issue.

Three, the rare stuff: infection, a fracture, a tumor. I think those are 
unlikely, and I'm not guessing. But night pain is the symptom that makes me 
rule them out instead of assuming.

If the scan shows wear and tear, that's useful too. We stop worrying and treat 
it as a back problem, with physical therapy and a better plan for the mattress.

It's your call, and I won't push you into it. But if you get new weakness in 
that leg, numbness in your groin or saddle area, or any trouble with your 
bladder or bowels, don't wait to think about it. Come straight in.

So what are you deciding?
Dale Kowalczyk: Okay. That's the first time anybody's told me what they're 
looking for before they went looking. Usually it's "let's get a picture and 
see."

Adjacent level, hardware, and the rare stuff you say is unlikely. I heard all 
three. And I heard the part about the leg and the bathroom.

I'll think about it. Not stalling. I just don't say yes in the room. One 
question, though: if the scan shows the hardware's a mess, what are you telling
me? Because "passing me to another subcontractor" is how this went last time.
Dr. Renata Okafor-Lindqvist: Fair question. I'll give you the straight version,
including what I can't do.

One, I'd tell you what the scan shows in plain words, and I'd show you the 
images if you want to see them. Two, I'm an emergency doctor, not a spine 
surgeon. I can tell you whether something looks urgent and whether it needs a 
specialist, but I won't pretend to decide on a re-operation from the ER. Three,
if it does need a surgeon, I make the call myself and send the images and my 
notes with you, so you're not starting from zero and retelling the story to a 
stranger.

I can't promise it won't end with a surgeon in the picture. I can promise you 
won't leave with a "good luck" and a phone number.

If anyone offers you some new device or technique, ask them who studied it, how
many patients, and how long they followed them. Ask them what the downsides 
are, too. I'd ask the same.

Take your time. You don't have to say yes in the room, but don't let "later" 
turn into "never" if that leg keeps changing. And don't wait on the bladder or 
groin symptoms. That's an all-caps rule.
Dale Kowalczyk: You're the first one who's said what she can't do. Most people 
in your line of work only tell you what they can.

"Who studied it, how many patients, how long." I'm writing that down somewhere.
Wish I'd had it in 2017. All I got was "ninety percent better," and nobody ever
told me ninety percent of what.

Groin, bladder, bowels, weak leg. I've got it. All caps.

I'm still going to think about it. But you can put the order in the system, and
if I show up for it, I show up. If I don't, I don't. My daughter's going to ask
me what you said, and I'd rather tell her I didn't walk out on you.
